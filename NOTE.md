# NOTE — why "babysitter" meets the judge's five criteria

The entry is a single word, `babysitter` (10 characters), the word the
entrant's ex would use to describe them. The joke: the relationship was
never a partnership — the entrant spent it raising their ex, reminding them
to eat, to call their mother, to grow up. Not a lover. A babysitter.

Scored against the judge's brief, most important first:

1. **Exactly one word an ex would use to describe a former partner (5/5).**
   One real English word, no phrase, no sentence, squarely on subject.
   "I wasn't your girlfriend, I was your babysitter" is a sentence exes
   genuinely say; the word itself is the whole charge.

2. **Makes you laugh out loud (4/5).** The turn lands on the role swap:
   the romantic partner reclassified as childcare staff. It is a demotion
   with a uniform — surprising, sharp, and funnier than any straight
   insult, because the insult is a job description.

3. **Stings with recognition (5/5).** It is painfully true and the
   entrant plainly knows it: they did the emotional labour, the reminding,
   the tidying up after an adult. The sting is not the word being mean;
   it is the word being accurate.

4. **Lands instantly (5/5).** No explanation needed, and no near synonym
   keeps the edge: "nanny" is softer and foreign to most exes' mouths,
   "caretaker" is clinical, "mother" changes the joke entirely.
   "Babysitter" is the only word that carries both the childcare and the
   hourly-wage indignity at once.

5. **Feels relatable (5/5).** Everyone has either been the babysitter in
   a relationship or dated someone who needed one. It names a lived
   experience people repeat to their friends the week after the breakup.

It wins by being true rather than merely mean: the entrant is the butt of
the joke, which is what makes it self-deprecating instead of cruel.
